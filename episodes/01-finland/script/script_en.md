# Episode 01 — The World Copied Finland's Schools. Then Finland Fell Apart.

## COLD OPEN

**P01** In the early 2000s, education delegations from around the world started flying to Helsinki. Finland's national education agency alone has hosted more than sixteen thousand visitors. Today it charges one thousand four hundred and eighty-five euros for a single school visit.
refs: F-VIS-1, F-VIS-2

**P02** They came to see a miracle. In 2006, Finnish fifteen-year-olds scored 548 in maths, on the world's largest school test.
refs: F-FIN-M-2006

**P03** Then it fell. Every round since has come in lower than the last. The latest score: 469.
refs: F-FIN-M-2006..2025 (each round lower than the last)

**P04** That's 79 points. By the OECD's own yardstick, roughly three years of learning, gone.
refs: F-FIN-DROP-M, F-OECD-YARD, F-DERIV-YEARS

**P05** The usual suspects are phones, the pandemic and immigration. None of them explains it. The real answer is stranger. The world copied Finland for the wrong reasons, and Finland may have made the same mistake about itself.
refs: —

## SETUP

**P06** The test is PISA, run by the OECD. Roughly every three years, it measures fifteen-year-olds in reading, maths and science. The 2025 round covered ninety-one countries and economies.
refs: F-PISA-SCALE

**P07** It doesn't test what students memorised. It tests whether they can use it on problems they've never seen. A bad result can end a minister's career. A good one can launch a consulting industry.
refs: —

## ACT 1 — THE MIRACLE

**P09** The first results came out in December 2001, and Finland was top of the world in reading. By 2006 it was scoring 563 in science, 548 in maths and 547 in reading.
refs: F-PISA2000, F-FIN-S-2006, F-FIN-M-2006, F-FIN-R-PEAK

**P10** Visitors found a system that seemed to break every rule. School starts at seven. No national tests until the end of upper secondary. No school inspectors. And every primary teacher holds a master's degree.
refs: F-FEAT-AGE, F-FEAT-TEST, F-DECENT, F-FEAT-MA

**P11** All of that was true. The mistake was one word: because. Finland tested less and scored more, so the world concluded it scored more because it tested less.
refs: —

**P13** But look at who sat the 2006 test. Born around 1991, they started school around 1998. The famous freedoms had only been written into the rules in the 1990s, and many of their teachers had trained in an older, far more centralised system. Rules change in a year. Classrooms change over decades.
refs: F-COHORT-2006, F-DECENT

## ACT 2 — THE FALL

**P14** Here is the whole maths record. A small climb to the 2006 peak, then a lower score in every round since. This isn't a blip. It's a trend that has run for almost twenty years.
refs: F-FIN-M-2003..2025

**P15** Reading followed the same path, from 547 down to 474, now joint seventeenth with Italy. Science held up best, but still slid from 563 to 504. Finland is still above the OECD average in all three. In maths, by just six points.
refs: F-FIN-R-PEAK, F-FIN-R-2025, F-FIN-R-RANK-2025, F-FIN-S-2006, F-FIN-S-2025, F-GAP-2025

**P16** Part of this is global. Across the OECD, reading fell 28 points and maths 22 between 2015 and 2025. But Finland fell further, and the damage shows at both ends.
refs: F-OECD-TREND

**P17** In the early 2000s, about seven per cent of Finnish students were below the baseline in maths. By 2022, one in four. The top shrank too. In 2003, more than twenty-three per cent reached the highest levels. In 2025, seven per cent.
refs: F-FIN-LOW-2006, F-FIN-LOW-2022, F-FIN-TOP-2003, F-FIN-TOP-2025

**P19** Now the neighbour. Estonia sits just across the Gulf of Finland, and its language is a close relative of Finnish. In 2006, it trailed Finland in maths by thirty-three points. By 2012, the two were level. In 2025, Estonia led by thirty-nine, and it was ahead in reading and science too.
refs: F-EST-GAP-2006, F-EST-M-2012, F-FIN-M-2012, F-EST-2025, F-FIN-M-2025, F-EST-LEAD-2025

**P21** And the top of the OECD table in 2025? Japan, first in all three subjects for the first time. So should everyone just copy East Asia? Hold that thought.
refs: F-JPN-RANK

## ACT 3 — WHY?

**P22** Six explanations get the blame. A good one has to explain three things: why Finland fell, why it fell further than most, and why it started after 2006.
refs: —

**P23** Suspect one: immigration. Scores fell among students with and without a migrant background. In maths, immigrant-background students held roughly steady since 2012, and the gap actually narrowed. Not the main cause.
refs: F-FIN-IMM-1, F-FIN-IMM-2

**P24** Suspect two: phones and the pandemic. The OECD links heavy leisure use of devices at school to lower scores. But that's a correlation, it's global, and Finland's slide began more than a decade before COVID-19. An accelerant, not the origin.
refs: F-DIG-1, F-FIN-M-2006..2025

**P25** Suspect three: reading. Finland's own education agency says reading used to be a popular pastime that held results up. Between 2000 and 2009, the share of Finnish students reading for enjoyment fell faster than the OECD average. And every maths question is also a reading test. A likely contributor.
refs: F-OPH-READ, F-READ-ENJ

**P26** Suspect four: student-led learning. A new curriculum built around phenomenon-based learning reached schools in 2016. But scores had already been falling for a decade. Possible, not proven.
refs: F-CUR-2014, F-FIN-M-2006..2025

**P27** Suspect five: inequality. More low performers, fewer top performers, wider gaps. All true. But that describes the damage. It doesn't explain it.
refs: F-FIN-DIFF

**P28** Suspect six: the legacy. In 2015, the economist Gabriel Heller Sahlgren argued that Finland's rise began before its celebrated reforms took effect, and its decline began soon after they did. The miracle, he said, came from something older: teacher-led classrooms and deep cultural roots.
refs: F-HS-2015

**P29** Now shift every maths score back eight years, to when those students started school. The best results belong to children who started in the late 1990s. The decline begins with those who started in the early 2000s. That doesn't prove the theory. But no other suspect fits the timing as well.
refs: F-COHORT, F-FIN-M-2003..2025, F-CUR-2014

## COUNTERPOINT

**P30** So is East Asia the answer? Japan tops the OECD, yet only sixty-three per cent of its students say they're curious about many things, against seventy-three across the OECD. Forty-eight per cent say they push harder when work gets difficult. The OECD average is sixty.
refs: F-JPN-CUR, F-JPN-PER

**P31** Critics like the education scholar Yong Zhao argue that test-driven systems can buy high scores at the cost of creativity. One test on one day is not a verdict on a nation.
refs: F-ZHAO

**P32** And Finland is still above average in every subject. Sliding from the top of the reading table to joint seventeenth is a crisis in Helsinki. In most capitals, it would be a press release.
refs: F-OECD-2025, F-PISA2000, F-FIN-R-RANK-2025

## RESOLUTION

**P33** Finland's success was real. But the world copied what it could see: the late start, the missing tests, the freedom. It missed what it couldn't see: a generation that read for pleasure, and teachers who taught the basics directly. Those couldn't be exported, and at home they quietly faded.
refs: F-HS-2015, F-OPH-READ, F-READ-ENJ

**P34** So the next time a headline announces an education miracle, ask three questions. When were these students actually taught? What changed before the scores did? And what's happening at the bottom?

# Episode 01 — The World Copied Finland's Schools. Then Finland Fell Apart.

Format: each paragraph is `**Pxx**` + narration, followed by `refs:` (fact IDs from `research/facts.md`).
The TTS pipeline reads only the narration lines.

---

## COLD OPEN

**P01** In the early 2000s, a small Nordic country became a place of pilgrimage. Education ministers, school principals and consultants flew to Helsinki to find out what Finland knew that they didn't. The national education agency alone has hosted more than sixteen thousand visitors. Eventually, Finland started charging admission. A school visit now costs one thousand four hundred and eighty-five euros. Plus VAT.
refs: F-VIS-1, F-VIS-2

**P02** They came to see a miracle. In 2006, Finnish fifteen-year-olds scored 548 points in mathematics on the world's largest school test. For reformers everywhere, that number became a destination.
refs: F-FIN-M-2006

**P03** Now watch what happened next. The score slipped, then kept sliding. Not one PISA round since 2006 has gone Finland's way. In the results published in September 2026, it reached 469.
refs: F-FIN-M-2006..2025 (each round lower than the last)

**P04** That is a fall of 79 points. By the OECD's own rough yardstick, about 22 points is a year of learning. So today's Finnish fifteen-year-olds are, very roughly, three years of maths behind the teenagers of 2006.
refs: F-FIN-DROP-M, F-OECD-YARD, F-DERIV-YEARS

**P05** The usual explanations are phones, the pandemic, and immigration. Each of them is either wrong or incomplete. The better answer is stranger. The world may have copied Finland for the wrong reasons. And Finland, it turns out, may have made the same mistake about itself. Let's test every suspect against the data, and see which ones survive.
refs: —

## SETUP

**P06** First, the scoreboard. PISA is the OECD's Programme for International Student Assessment. Roughly every three years since 2000, it tests fifteen-year-olds in reading, mathematics and science. The 2025 round covered ninety-one countries and economies, and about seven hundred and sixty thousand students. It's not a perfect instrument, and we'll get to its limits later.
refs: F-PISA-SCALE

**P07** PISA doesn't ask students to recite a syllabus. It asks whether they can use what they know on problems they haven't seen before. That makes it hard to cram for, and easy to headline. A bad PISA result can sink an education minister. A good one can launch a consulting industry.
refs: —

**P08** On the PISA scale, a gap of ten or fifteen points is already meaningful. The OECD reckons about twenty-two points is a year of school. A gap of eighty is a different world.
refs: F-OECD-YARD

## ACT 1 — THE MIRACLE

**P09** When the first PISA results came out in December 2001, Finland was top of the world in reading, with 546 points. By 2006, the numbers were even more striking: 547 in reading, 548 in maths, and 563 in science.
refs: F-PISA2000, F-FIN-R-PEAK, F-FIN-M-2006, F-FIN-S-2006

**P10** The visitors found a system that looked almost designed to embarrass everyone else's reforms. Children didn't start school until seven. There was no national standardised test until the very end of upper secondary school. School inspectors had been abolished. And every primary school teacher needed a master's degree.
refs: F-FEAT-AGE, F-FEAT-TEST, F-DECENT, F-FEAT-MA

**P11** Here was a country that tested less, started later and trusted teachers more, and it still won. For reformers who were tired of exams and league tables, it was the perfect story. Finland became shorthand for a whole philosophy of schooling, quoted by people who had never sat in a Finnish classroom.
refs: —

**P12** None of those features was invented. They were real. The problem is a single word: because. Finland had few tests, and Finland scored highly. The world concluded that Finland scored highly because it had few tests.
refs: —

**P13** But think about who actually sat the test in 2006. They were fifteen, so they were born around 1991. They started school at seven, around 1998. Much of the freedom the visitors admired had only been written into the rules in the 1990s, when inspections and centrally approved materials disappeared and schools began writing their own curricula. Rules can change in a year. Classrooms change over decades. Many of the teachers in front of those high scorers had trained and worked for years in an older, far more centralised system. Keep that date in mind. We'll come back to it.
refs: F-COHORT, F-DECENT

## ACT 2 — THE FALL

**P14** Here is the whole maths record. A small climb to the 2006 peak, then a lower score in every round since. The slope isn't a blip. It's a trend that has run for almost twenty years.
refs: F-FIN-M-2003..2025

**P15** Reading tells the same story. From a peak of 547 in 2006 to 474 today, level with Italy, in joint seventeenth place. Science has held up best: 563 in 2006, 504 now, twelfth in the world. The fall is real, but so is the altitude. Finland is still above the OECD average in all three subjects.
refs: F-FIN-R-PEAK, F-FIN-R-2025, F-FIN-R-RANK-2025, F-FIN-S-2006, F-FIN-S-2025, F-FIN-S-RANK-2025, F-OECD-2025

**P15b** Just not by much. In maths, the OECD average in 2025 was 463. Finland scored 469. The country that the world once flew in to study now sits six points from the middle of the table. In reading, the gap is thirteen points. Only science still looks like the old Finland.
refs: F-OECD-2025, F-FIN-M-2025, F-FIN-R-2025

**P16** And Finland is not falling alone. Across the OECD, average reading scores dropped 28 points between 2015 and 2025, which is about a year and a half of learning. Maths dropped 22. Between 2018 and 2022, the OECD average in maths fell fifteen points, the largest drop on record. Part of Finland's story is simply everyone's story.
refs: F-OECD-TREND, F-2022-OECDΔ

**P17** But only part. An average can hide a lot, so let's split it open. In the early 2000s, about seven per cent of Finnish students scored below the baseline level in maths. By 2022 it was one in four. Since 2015, the share of low performers has risen by seventeen percentage points in maths and fifteen in reading. In reading today, more than one Finnish student in four is below the baseline. Among boys, it's about one in three.
refs: F-FIN-LOW-2006, F-FIN-LOW-2022, F-FIN-LOW-Δ2015, F-FIN-LOW-R-2025

**P18** The top has thinned out too. In 2003, more than twenty-three per cent of Finnish students reached the highest levels in maths. In 2025, seven per cent did, which is now below the OECD average. Finland didn't just lose its floor. It lost its ceiling as well.
refs: F-FIN-TOP-2003, F-FIN-TOP-2025

**P19** Now bring in the neighbour. Estonia sits just across the Gulf of Finland, and its language is a close relative of Finnish. In 2006, Estonia trailed Finland in maths by thirty-three points. By 2012, the two were level: 521 to 519, a statistical tie. Since then, the lines have only moved apart.
refs: F-EST-M-2006, F-FIN-M-2006, F-EST-M-2012, F-FIN-M-2012

**P20** In 2025, Estonia scored 508 in maths against Finland's 469. It's ahead in science, 527 to 504, and in reading, 499 to 474. Estonia is now Europe's best performer in maths and science. To be fair, Estonia's scores have slipped since 2018 too. It's falling more slowly, and from higher up.
refs: F-EST-2025, F-FIN-M-2025, F-FIN-S-2025, F-FIN-R-2025, F-EST-RANK, F-EST-DECL

**P21** And at the top of the OECD table, there's a name that rarely came up in the Finland story. In 2025, Japan ranked first among OECD members in reading, maths and science, the first time it has done so since PISA began. Back in 2022, when maths scores fell almost everywhere, Japan and Korea were the only OECD members that improved. So is the lesson simply, "be more like East Asia"? Hold that thought.
refs: F-JPN-RANK, F-JPN-2025, F-2022-JPKR

## ACT 3 — WHY?

**P22** There are six popular explanations for Finland's decline. A good explanation has to account for three things. Why Finland fell. Why it fell further than most of the OECD. And why it started when it did, after 2006. Let's take the suspects one at a time, and give each a verdict.
refs: —

**P23** Suspect one: immigration. Finland has become more diverse, and immigrant-background students do score lower on average. But if immigration explained the fall, the scores of everyone else would have held steady. They didn't. Results fell among students with and without a migrant background. In maths, immigrant-background students were roughly where they had been in 2012. It was everyone else who fell, and the gap between the groups actually narrowed. Verdict: not the main cause.
refs: F-FIN-IMM-1, F-FIN-IMM-2, F-FIN-IMM-3

**P24** Suspect two: phones and the pandemic. The OECD's 2025 report finds that students who use devices heavily, or for leisure at school, tend to score noticeably lower. More than one student in four says classmates are distracted by devices in most science lessons. That's a correlation, not proof, but it's a strong one. The problem is timing. Finland's slide began after 2006, more than a decade before anyone had heard of COVID-19. And phones are everywhere, including in Estonia and Japan. Verdict: an accelerant, not the origin.
refs: F-DIG-1, F-DIG-2, F-FIN-M-2009, F-FIN-M-2012

**P25** Suspect three: reading. Finland's own education agency says that reading used to be a popular pastime, and that this habit held up results across the board. Social media and gaming now take that time. The OECD found that the share of Finnish students who read for enjoyment fell between 2000 and 2009, faster than the OECD average. Reading isn't just one subject. Every maths and science question in PISA is also a reading test. A student who struggles to read the problem never gets to the maths. It would also fit the shape of the decline, which has been concentrated among weaker students and, in reading, among boys. Verdict: a likely contributor.
refs: F-OPH-READ, F-READ-ENJ, F-FIN-LOW-R-2025

**P26** Suspect four: student-led learning. In 2014, Finland published a new core curriculum built around cross-disciplinary, phenomenon-based learning. Schools introduced it from August 2016. It's a tempting culprit, but look at the calendar. Scores had been falling for a decade before it arrived, so it can't have started the decline. The 2025 test-takers are roughly the first to have had it for their entire school career. Whether it deepened the fall is still argued over in Finland. Verdict: possible, not proven.
refs: F-CUR-2014, F-FIN-M-2009..2022, F-COHORT

**P27** Suspect five: inequality. The Finnish government's own summary of 2025 says the same thing three ways. More low performers. Fewer top performers. Wider gaps between students. That's true, and it matters. But it's less a cause than a description. It tells us where the damage landed. It doesn't tell us what did the damage.
refs: F-FIN-DIFF

**P28** Which brings us to suspect six: the legacy. In 2015, the economist Gabriel Heller Sahlgren published a report called Real Finnish Lessons. His argument was uncomfortable. Finland's rise, he wrote, began before most of its celebrated reforms could take effect, and its decline began soon after they took hold. What produced the miracle was older: a hierarchical, teacher-led classroom culture, and deep historical and cultural roots.
refs: F-HS-2015

**P29** Remember 1998, the year our peak test-takers started school? We can test the timing ourselves. Take every Finnish maths score and slide it back eight years, to roughly when those students started school. Now the chart tells a different story. The best results belong to children who entered school in the mid-to-late 1990s. The decline begins with the cohorts who started in the early 2000s. And the 2016 curriculum only arrives at the very end of the line. This doesn't prove the legacy theory. Relabelling an axis never proves anything. But of all six suspects, this one fits the timing best.
refs: F-COHORT, F-FIN-M-2003..2025, F-CUR-2014

## COUNTERPOINT

**P30** So should everyone just copy Japan instead? Not so fast. In the same 2025 survey, sixty-three per cent of Japanese students said they were curious about many different things. The OECD average was seventy-three. Forty-eight per cent said they put in extra effort when work gets difficult, against an OECD average of sixty. Self-reported answers vary with culture, and modesty is part of the culture here. But these are not the numbers of a system that has solved education.
refs: F-JPN-CUR, F-JPN-PER

**P31** Critics like the education scholar Yong Zhao have spent years arguing that PISA measures a narrow slice of what schools do, and that test-driven systems can produce high scores while crushing creativity. You don't have to agree with him completely to accept the basic point. A test of fifteen-year-olds on one day is not a verdict on a nation.
refs: F-ZHAO

**P32** And perspective matters. Finland is still above the OECD average in every subject. It's twelfth in the world in science. Sliding from the top of the reading table to joint seventeenth is a national crisis in Helsinki. In most capitals, it would be a press release.
refs: F-OECD-2025, F-FIN-S-RANK-2025, F-PISA2000, F-FIN-R-RANK-2025

## RESOLUTION

**P33** So what happened? Finland's success in the 2000s was real. But the world, and in time Finland itself, misread what caused it. Visitors saw the visible features: the late start, the missing tests, the freedom. They missed the invisible ones: a generation that read for pleasure, and classrooms where teachers taught the basics carefully and directly. The visible features could be exported. The invisible ones couldn't, and at home, they quietly faded.
refs: F-HS-2015, F-OPH-READ, F-READ-ENJ

**P34** That's the real lesson, and it isn't about Finland. It's about how we read success. We tend to credit whatever is easiest to see. So the next time a headline announces an education miracle, ask three questions. When were these students actually taught? What changed before the scores did? And what's happening to the bottom quarter? If a story can't answer those, it's a brochure, not an explanation. Finland spent twenty years being the answer to a question nobody had asked carefully.
